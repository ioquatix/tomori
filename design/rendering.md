# Rendering Order

This describes how the scene's surfaces are ordered when they are drawn, so that what is nearer the eye is seen in front, how what is drawn on them is drawn: text as text, and shapes as geometry, what of a projected panel is laid out and meshed once and kept across frames, what is culled because neither eye can see it, and what a frame costs the CPU and the GPU.

## Requirement

**Nearer is in front.** In each eye, whatever is nearer the eye is seen in front of whatever is farther, whatever order they are drawn in. This holds between the scene's opaque bodies, the swarm, workers, the forms of arrival's objects and the hand menu's tiles, the ray, the landing ring, and the controllers and hands, and the translucent surfaces with text on them: projection walls and panels, the outline of the slot a panel carried would take, a wall's grab bar, handles, padlock and hint, the label on the ray, the hint beside a panel carried, the GitHub inbox and its cards, the status pill, the recording indicator, the `LIVE` badge, the right wrist's transcript, the hand menu's and arrival's labels, and workers' signs. The hand menu, cards, the recording indicator, the hint, the pill and the controllers cover a panel behind them; a panel covers any of them behind it. Text is drawn with the depth test like everything else: nothing is drawn as an overlay, always on top, except the teleport's blink, which darkens the whole view.

**How.** Opaque bodies are drawn first and write depth. Translucent surfaces are then drawn with the depth test, without writing depth, each blended over what is already behind it. Once a surface is drawn, its backing, the quad behind its text, is drawn again into depth alone, where it is at least 60% opaque (`occlusion::OCCLUDING`): whatever is behind it is then hidden even if it is drawn later, and whatever is in front of it still covers it. Depth is written fragment by fragment, not quad by quad: a fragment of a backing less than 60% opaque, as its colour's alpha and its coverage of the atlas make it, is discarded, so that a rounded corner's outside, a glyph's margin, the outside of any shape from the shared primitives and an antialiased edge write no depth, though drawn in the same quad as what is opaque, and a translucent surface drawn after them behind them still shows there. Every pipeline that writes a translucent surface's depth, the panels' and the signs', does so. A surface fainter than that writes no depth, so that nothing vanishes behind what can barely be seen: a wall's board, a curved wall's slot boards, the outline of a slot, and a label or sign fading in or out. Arrival's labels, the hand menu's in a space, are drawn last, farthest first among themselves, so that nothing drawn after them needs their depth: they write none, sparing the headset a draw for each label in each eye. A surface fainter than 60% is blended over what is drawn before it, so it is drawn after what stands behind it, as below.

**Near and far.** Every client's eyes, the headset's, the desktop window's and the spectator's, see from 5 cm to 2 km (`tomori_scene::depth`), into a 32-bit float depth buffer with WebGPU's depth from 0 to 1, nearer less. The far plane is a plane, so it cuts what is off to the side nearer than what is straight ahead; at 50 m, as it was until November 2026, the farmyard's farthest hills and bush blinked in and out as the head turned. At 2 km it is far beyond any scenery, which an environment's haze hides first: the farmyard's, from anywhere within the space's bounds, all of it lies within 200 m, where its haze has hidden it to a thousandth. Depth near the eyes is decided by the near plane, at about `1 - near / distance` whatever the far, so moving the far plane changed it by less than a tenth of a percent, and `occlusion::TIE` with it. Culling's frustum is the eyes' own, far plane and all. The headset submits no depth to the compositor (see below), so nothing else needs the range.

**Order between surfaces about as far away.** A backing's depth is written a little behind it, `occlusion::TIE`, about 2% of its distance for each metre of it: 4 mm at arm's length, 2 cm at 1 m and 11 cm at 2.3 m. Between surfaces closer in depth than that, the order they are drawn in decides, which the frame step chooses: the space's walls and floating panels, each wall with its panels, farthest first, each wall's panels after the wall and the outline of a slot over the panel in it; the label on the ray and then the hint beside a panel carried; the inbox and cards; then the pill, the recording indicator, the badge and the transcript; and arrival's labels, in a space the hand menu's, last. Workers' signs are ordered among themselves by where they overlap, as seen from each eye, and drawn among the space's walls and panels back to front from that eye: each list keeps its own order, and between the two the one whose next surface is farther from the eye, by where its middle is, goes first, so that a translucent wall behind a sign is drawn before the sign and a sign fading in front of a wall blends over it, and a sign behind a wall is drawn before the wall.

**Text is drawn as text; shapes as geometry, from shared primitives.** Every line, rule, box, frame, bar, tick, dot or other indicator that a panel, card, menu, hint, label or sign draws is drawn as a shape, from the primitives in `tomori_scene::shapes`, rather than as glyphs: a rule (`Shape::Rule`), a box, filled, rounded or a frame (`Shape::Rect`), a filled area that meets its neighbours exactly (`Shape::Fill`), a bar with its track (`Shape::Bar`), a dot (`Shape::Disc`), a ring (`Shape::Ring`), a checkbox (`Shape::Check`), any four-sided area filled to its edges (`Shape::Quad`), as a chart shades under and between its lines, a segment of a line drawn as one piece (`Shape::Stroke`) and a small dot drawn as one (`Shape::Dot`), as a chart's lines and lone points are, so that a chart of many points fits a panel's buffer. A shape is placed in font pixels, the units text is laid out in, so that layout mixes the two; layout that lays text out in cells marks a cell to be drawn as a shape (`Mark`), keeping a character that stands in for it, which the console prints, and a run of a line's cells along a row or down a column is drawn as one line. Shapes are drawn in the same mesh as their panel's text, by the same pipeline and shader, so that they add no draw call, are tested against depth as text is, and are drawn alike in both eyes. Each is drawn from distance fields in the glyph atlas, a square, a disc, a ring, a frame and a wedge, so that its edges are antialiased as a glyph's are, but for two filled to their very edges, which are not: `Shape::Fill`, a background or where an image will be, which meets its neighbours without a seam or lies under what covers it, and `Shape::Quad`, a chart's shaded area, whose columns reach the lines that bound it at their peaks; and a `Shape::Stroke` is antialiased across its length but cut square at its ends, which its neighbours overlap; it is as bright all along, with no seam where its pieces meet, and a line thinner than a pixel, far away, keeps its ink as it moves rather than shimmering. **Charts are drawn from the same primitives, in their panel's mesh.** A chart panel's chart (see `projection.md`) is laid out by `tomori_scene::chart` into shapes and labels, text placed anywhere on a canvas (`canvas::Label`), drawn from the glyph atlas as a row's text is, so that a chart is tested against depth, drawn alike in both eyes and follows a curved wall's slot as text does, at no draw call of its own: each segment of a line, at any angle, is a stroke turned along it, one piece, whose field across it the shader antialiases, as a rule's middle is, so that a thin line far away keeps its ink as it moves, as a rule does; segments that meet overlap by half the line's width where they turn by a right angle or less, so that a joint, even a step's, shows no notch, without a dot at each joint, and meet at a dot of one piece where they turn more sharply, where overlapping ends would notch the outside of the turn or overshoot it, at most 64 a series; the overlap of opaque lines is not seen; a lone point is a dot of one piece; dashes are short strokes; bars are filled boxes; and a shaded area is drawn in pieces, at most 512 quads a chart, each where its edges have a gap throughout within one of equal columns across the plot, so that nothing is shaded where the edges meet or cross: a piece is a quad reaching the widest gap within it, both edges there, a spike's peak included, level there and sloping to its ends, or two meeting at the peak, so that it shades the area between its edges within it, filled to its edges; a piece of a step series' gap is one level quad as tall as the gap on average. Between a piece's peak and its ends its top is straight, so it lies along a line that bends within it only as near as a column's width allows. Text keeps symbols that are text: an arrow or a bullet inline in a sentence, a tick beside a word, an icon that draws cleanly as one character, and box drawing in content, such as a code block's. The characters that may still draw a shape as text are listed, with why, in `crates/scene/tests/shapes_in_text.rs`, which fails for any other.

**One composition layer.** The headset draws everything into the one OpenXR projection layer it submits, both eyes' images with the scene's depth, and submits no quad layers: the compositor would draw layers in their order, not by depth, so a quad layer drawn over the projection layer would cover a hand in front of it. Text is kept sharp within that layer, by the glyph atlas and multisampling (see `text.md` and `desktop.md`). Moving panels into quad layers for sharpness would revise this requirement, and would need the hand menu and every other near surface in later layers, with depth submitted through `XR_KHR_composition_layer_depth` for the runtime to hide them by depth.

**A panel is laid out and meshed once for what it shows.** A projected panel's face is laid out once for what the panel shows and the size it is drawn at, and kept across frames, by `tomori-scene`'s `projection::faces`: its Markdown parsed and wrapped into rows, its tables, checklists and quotes among them, or its code laid out with its highlighting, its chart or its image placed, how large it is and how far it scrolls, and the face drawn from them, a canvas shared with the renderer rather than copied. The renderer keeps each face's mesh, its glyphs' and shapes' quads in the panel's own plane, about its middle, in a GPU buffer, uploaded once, for as long as it is handed the same face, which it knows by its pointer without comparing it, wherever the face is drawn among the others. Nothing else a frame changes lays a face out or meshes it again: a wall moved, tilted, bent or travelling, the head moving, signs turning, the ray sweeping and where it ends each move a face's model alone, one matrix per eye; a curved wall's slots are each flat, with a frame of their own, so its curve is in the models too. What does: what the panel shows changing, as the server's patch upserts it, or the projection whole, which lays that panel out again; its chart's data alone changing, which draws its face again from the layout kept; its size, a slot of another shape or scale or floating, each of the latest three sizes kept; scrolling a whole row, which draws its face again from its layout, a table's pinned header and a code panel's whole lines as before, and builds its mesh, for whoever scrolls it alone; and its frame's colour, as it is pointed at or carried, or becomes or stops being the newest on its wall, which rewrites only its frame's vertices in the mesh kept, with no other draw. A glyph not yet in the atlas builds its mesh again until it is, as `text.md` says. The key, from a server that versions panels, is the panel's version, the projection's revision of its last change, and for a chart its data seq, the revision its data last changed at (see `projection.md`): a new version lays it out again, and a new data seq at the same version draws its chart's face again, its summary's rows followed, without laying it out; with what the client changes of a panel itself, which no version follows, its body and code, as a tick shown at once and a code panel sent short or whole change them, its title, and its age as it shows, in minutes, hours or days, rather than the seconds the client counts on for every panel at each patch, so that a patch lays out only the panels it changes. From an older server, which versions nothing, or which counted each text panel's changes as its revision but none its environment made, as a chart panel with no data seq shows, the key is the panel as the projection has it, compared whole once a frame, its age as it shows, so that every field shown is followed. A face not drawn keeps its buffer among eight spare, the latest drawn, for faces to come; beyond them its buffers are freed, and a panel no longer projected has its layouts forgotten at once. **Nothing unchanged is drawn or meshed again as the order changes.** The surfaces about a panel are kept the same way, each by what it is drawn from: a wall's own faces, by `projection::walls`, a flat wall's board by its layout wherever it stands, a curved wall's one slot board, which every slot of it shows, by its frame's colour and its slots' cells, and its label by its text, a flat wall's of a group by it and the other walls of its group as they stand, and the outline of a slot by its cells, each forgotten once its wall is not shown; each wall's grab bar, drawn at one size whatever its wall's and scaled by its model, by its length in font pixels and whether it is highlighted; each handle's face of the wall taken hold of, drawn a handle across and scaled by its model, by its handle, whether it is highlighted and whether its wall is locked, whichever wall shows it; a group's boards kept as the group is carried, its walls standing as they stood from each other; the label on the ray by what it says, its panel's title, metadata, code, place and age as it shows, how it scrolls and the item it names; and the GitHub inbox and each card, by what the inbox says and by the card wherever it stands, covered or highlighted, forgotten once it leaves the board. What each is drawn from is compared in place with what it was drawn from, never copied or drawn to be compared, so that one unchanged costs a comparison of a few fields a frame. The renderer matches each card it draws to the face it is handed by that face's identity, its canvas's pointer, never by its place in the draw order, and a face drawn by several sheets at once, as a curved wall's slot board, by as many cards, each keeping its mesh: so when the walls, the panels and the cards change their order by distance from the head, as it turns, only the order they are drawn in changes, still farthest first, and nothing is drawn, built or uploaded again. What still is, each frame it is shown, is what changes as the head moves: the hint beside a panel carried, the hint above a wall taken hold of and the label beside a bar the ray meets, each sized to its distance, and the label beside a teleport pad, each a few quads; a wall's handles stand where its frame puts them, whatever the head does (see `projection.md`). As the ray moves across a chart's plot, its readout is drawn each frame (see `projection.md`), a surface of its own in the face's plane, over the face, drawn from where the face's plot was drawn and the chart's values, never by laying the chart out or drawing its face again: a line, a dot or two for each series, a backing and at most five lines of 40 characters, from glyphs the atlas holds, kept while it shows the same.

**One haze for everything.** Everything drawn in the world fades with distance by the scene's one haze and nothing else: what is `d` metres from the eye fades into the environment's haze colour by `1 - exp(-density d²)`, the `haze_amount`, `hazed` and `hazed_glow` functions of `tomori-assets`' `haze.wgsl`, which each pipeline's shader is compiled after (`tomori_assets::haze::shader`). The density and colour are the environment's own, which its scenery says (`Scenery::haze`): the farmyard's evening haze, from its lighting, 0.0008, and Sky's night blue, 0.00025; the grid has none, nor does an environment growing out of a space's object until the eye is inside it (`Renderer::haze`). Tomori's cubes, among them the swarm, workers, the landing and home rings, pads, rays, volumes' solids and the others in a space (see `people.md`), its controllers, its signs, arrival's labels, panels, walls and their images, and the environments' models, ferns, water, plaques, platform and clouds, are hazed alike, from the eye's camera or each panel's uniform for each eye; a glow, as the bonfire and a marshmallow's trail of light, which adds its light, loses as much of it as the haze hides. Only what stands beyond the far plane, the sky itself and the farmyard's ranges, painted into the sky, is not, and no shader has a fade of its own. Lighting is unchanged: the scenery's models are lit by its light, and Tomori's own cubes and controllers by their own soft light from above, so that they look as they did up close; the others' swarms, among Tomori's cubes, glow, nearly unshaded by it, by a glow each instance carries (see `people.md`). Text is hazed too, with no floor of its own: at the farmyard's density a panel 5 m away fades by 2%, 10 m away by 8%, 20 m by 27% and 30 m by 51%, and Sky's by a fifth at 30 m; since both hazes are dark, as the panels' backings are, light text on its backing keeps a contrast of 15:1 at 10 m, 11:1 at 20 m and 7:1 at 30 m in the farmyard, from 16:1 up close, and at least 12:1 in Sky, so that a large wall read from across the yard dims but stays legible.

**Tomori's cubes are at least a pixel across.** A cube whose largest side would span less than a pixel (`SMALLEST`), however far away, is drawn a pixel across, by the eye's height in pixels in its camera, and dimmed by as much as its area grew, so that a particle never falls between a pixel's samples and goes missing, nor adds more light than it would; a cube spanning a pixel or more is drawn as it is.

**Volumes' solids are opaque bodies.** A volume's nodes, edges and groups are unit solids drawn by the swarm's instanced pipeline among the opaque bodies, writing depth, one draw for each solid, within a budget of triangles; its labels are small panels among the translucent surfaces, each backing writing its depth; what it draws is built once for each diagram and moved by its model. See `diagrams.md`.

**What a frame costs is logged.** The headset and the desktop window each time every frame on the frame thread (`tomori_scene::frame_timing::Timer`): the whole frame, on the headset from `xrWaitFrame` returning to `xrEndFrame` returning, and how much of it was busy, the frame less the waits within it, which is what the CPU's work cost; and each part of it they make, polling the connection, which applies patches and images and carries out what the frame asked for, asking for the microphone, locating the eyes, syncing and sampling input, the haptics, spatial audio, sending presence, the frame step, the projector within it, the renderer's update of what the frame changed and its encoding of the eyes' draws, on the headset their submission and beginning and ending the frame with the runtime apart, and the glyphs drawn into the atlas within any of them; and, apart, as waits rather than work, waiting for the GPU to finish the previous frame, for the compositor to give back an image, and on the headset in `xrWaitFrame`, before the frame, for the runtime to start it. They count the faces drawn, by the projector, the labels about it and the cards, the meshes the renderer built or recoloured, the glyphs drawn, and the projected panels' faces the renderer drew in the eyes and culled as neither eye could see them (`Renderer::panels_seen`), the panels alone, each face marked as one (`canvas::Kind::PanelFace`), not the walls' boards, labels, cards, the pill or the wrist's transcript drawn with them, so that the two add up to the panels shown, and those of them the projector handed over as their titles alone (below). Once a minute, beside the frame rate, they log at `info` one line of the mean, the 95th percentile and the most of each part timed, in milliseconds, then the waits, then each count a frame, then the panels' level of detail, `panel LOD angle` or `panel LOD off`, and last the longest part of polling the connection in that minute, what it was and how long it took: applying one event, by its kind, such as a `ProjectionPatch`, carrying out one action, such as `EndUtterance`, or the poll's own work before the events, `tick`, between them and the actions, `reopen`, or after them, `cues` (`tomori_client::connection::Slowest`), so that a long poll says what it was:

The desktop window logs the parts it has, prefixed by its frame rate, `Desktop frames, 59.8 fps; CPU over …`, or `fps —` before there is one; the parts overlap where one is within another, as the projector within the step and glyphs within whatever builds a mesh, so that a headset dropping frames shows where its frame thread goes.

**Only what the eyes may see is drawn.** Once a frame, before either eye is drawn, the renderer culls what neither eye can see in its render world (`tomori_world::World`) against one frustum enclosing both (`tomori_assets::bounds::Frustum::enclosing`): for each side, the plane of the two eyes' that holds both eyes' corners most tightly, moved out as far as it must be to hold them all, so that its apex lies behind the eyes where their outer planes meet. It is one test for both eyes and never culls what either sees; the desktop window and the spectator have one view and cull against it. Everything drawn has bounds in the world: a model's placed primitive, the box about its vertices moved by its node's matrix, or, skinned, as the hens are, the box about each joint's box of the vertices it moves at all, moved by that joint's matrix, which holds every pose without measuring one (`tomori_assets::bounds::Extent`), or none, never culled, for a skin whose weights do not sum to one; each instance batch, the box about its pieces, culled whole first, then piece by piece, its placements' buffer written again only when which it draws changes; each fern, about its kind's meshes as far as the breeze sways it; each of Tomori's own cubes, the swarm, workers, rings, pads and rays among them, its cube scaled, turned and moved; and each panel the frame step hands the renderer, and each of the farmyard's plaques, the box about its mesh and its image's quad in its own plane, culled with its backing's depth, the rest keeping the frame step's order; workers' signs and arrival's labels are not culled. Everything culled is submitted to the world, each item in its own coordinates, its root, which the frustum is carried into: Tomori's cubes, one dynamic item of an instance each, in the scene's, and each other person in the space one dynamic item of their own of their cubes, culled whole and then cube by cube, drawn with the rest in the same draw; the panels, one dynamic item, in the space's, the frame step's order kept; and what the environment's scenery submits, in its own coordinates, carried into the scene's, and the avatar's, in the scene's, which are culled only while they are drawn and forgotten when an avatar's scenery is dropped. Static instances, each the box of a piece that stays where it is until its environment changes, are found through a bounding volume hierarchy of them, built with binned surface area heuristics when they change, which finds exactly what testing each box finds; dynamic ones are tested one by one, each item's box about its instances first. Once what is placed stops growing, a frame of it allocates nothing. A scenery submits once a frame through `Scenery::submit`, after `Scenery::animate`, adding its items once and placing again what moved, and through `Scenery::cull`, after the world has culled and before its eyes are prepared, draws only what `Views::seen` says the eyes may see of them, saying how much it drew and culled: the farmyard's models, its upper terrace among them, ferns and plaques, and Sky's platform and shrine, with Sky's clouds an item never culled; it submits its lamps and moonlight too, for the light lists to come. One that submits nothing culls what it draws against the views' frustum itself, or draws everything, as before. What is not submitted yet is still drawn, unculled, where it was: the farmyard's water, sky, ranges, fire and trails, Sky's background, Tomori's controllers, workers' signs and arrival's labels; each other environment's scenery still culls itself, or not, as before. They move onto the world as the proposal's second stage goes on. A test that turns culling off (`Renderer::set_culling`) draws the same pixels.

**Large static models are drawn in chunks.** A scenery splits its static models as it uploads them, each primitive wider across the ground than a chunk into chunks along a grid (`Model::chunked`, `Primitive::chunks`), so that each chunk is a piece of its own, in the static instances' hierarchy, culled and lit on its own: its box across the ground cut evenly into cells at most a chunk across, each triangle in the cell its middle is in, but for a part of it that is closed, or no wider than half a chunk, kept whole in the cell its box's middle is in, so that a post or a stone is never cut and a closed part is still drawn without its back faces. No triangle is cut and no vertex added: the chunks' triangles are the very ones the primitive had, each corner as it was, so they meet without a seam and draw what it drew, but where which of two surfaces at one depth is drawn first decides a sample on a silhouette. A triangle longer than a cell makes its chunk as long, so what is drawn as one large face is drawn in small triangles first, as the farmyard's meadow and earth are (`farmyard.md`). A skinned mesh, bounded by its joints, is kept whole. A model's primitives share one vertex buffer and one index buffer, and a material is bound only as it changes, so that a chunk drawn after another of its primitive is one draw and nothing more. The farmyard's chunks are at most 7 m across (`tomori_environment_farmyard_client::scenery::CHUNK`): its yard, with its meadow and pines 43 by 45 m, in 31 and its fence's ten rails, its foothills, 72 m across, in 104, its trail in 10 and its upper terrace in 2; nothing else it draws is as large. Sky's platform and shrine, 9 by 10 m, lit by their baked light rather than by lamps, stay whole.

**Closed parts are drawn without their back faces.** A model's primitive is drawn in two parts: the triangles of its closed parts first, without their back faces, then the rest with them. A part, of triangles joined at their edges where their vertices are, is closed when every edge in it is shared by exactly two of its triangles crossing it in opposite directions, wound counterclockwise seen from outside, and it encloses a volume, its material hiding what is behind it rather than cut out (`Primitive::closed_triangles`); from outside it, what of it faces away from the eye is behind what of it faces the eye. An open part, a card drawn on both sides, a leaf, a frond, the water and every panel keep both faces; so does a mirrored instance, and everything while a scenery grows through the reveal sphere, which opens what it cuts. What changes is only which of a closed part's two faces wins the depth tie along its silhouette, where both meet: a sample or two of a few pixels in each eye, at most one in 2,000, shows its front face's shading rather than its back's. From inside a closed part, as with a head put into the campsite's log or the coop's roof, the part is not drawn at all, where with both faces its inside showed.

**The sky is drawn after the opaque scene.** An environment's scenery draws its opaque bodies first, nearest first, and anything as far as the sky after them, at the far plane, comparing `LessEqual` without writing depth, so that it is shaded only where nothing nearer covers it: the farmyard's sky after its water and before its ranges, Sky's after its platform and shrine. The pixels are those of the sky drawn first.

**Each instance is lit by its own few lights.** Once a frame, after culling, the render world chooses the lights that light each instance the eyes may see, by one function, `tomori_world::assign`, so that clustered lighting could replace it without changing environments or the lights they submit. Of a root's lights whose sphere of reach the views may see, at most 64 light its items (`tomori_world::LIGHTS`), of more the 64 nearest reaching the first eye, by how far each is from reaching it, less within its reach, kept in the order they were added (`World::root_lights`), those as near as the nearest left out fading out over the last fifth of their reach nearer than it, their intensity scaled down, so that a light passing another at the cut fades rather than coming or going at once, though one whose reach comes into the views or leaves them, among more than 64, still changes which are kept at once; of those, an instance is lit by each whose sphere meets its bounds, with a centimetre's margin, so that rounding never leaves out a light that lights some of it, at most 8 (`LIGHTS_PER_INSTANCE`), in the same order (`World::lit`). Of more than 8, the brightest at its bounds' centre are kept, each falling off as if it reached as much further as the bounds are large, so that every light meeting them ranks, and those near the cut fade out over the last fifth of their brightness above the brightest left out (`FADE`), so that a light passing another in brightness fades rather than flickers, and lights exactly as bright as one left out give nothing with it; ties go to the light added first. An instance that may be anywhere is lit by the 8 brightest, whole, and an empty one by none. The list is the instance's, all over it: one met by more than 8 lights goes without the rest even where they are nearest, so what is large is drawn in chunks (above), each with its own list. Choosing them allocates nothing once what is placed stops growing. Each pixel then adds up only its instance's lights: a scenery's lighting holds up to 64 lamps (`tomori_assets::gpu::LAMPS`), its root's lights in their order, and each piece of a model, and each of the farmyard's ferns, carries its own lamps in four words beside where it is placed (`Renderer::light`, `tomori_assets::gpu::lamps`): a mask of the lamps, by index, and how much of its light each gives, a byte each in the mask's order, 255 for all of it and past the last. A pixel loops over the mask in the lamps' order, reading nothing more than its piece, with each lamp's whole light unless a byte says otherwise, which it tells once for the piece. Each lamp's light is reckoned as before, and one that reaches none of a piece adds nothing to it, so within reach and within budget each lamp lights as when every pixel added every lamp, but where a GPU rounds the two loops a colour apart. A piece without a list, as a model drawn without a world, is lit by every lamp, as before.

**What a frame costs the GPU is logged.** Where the device has the GPU's timestamps, which the headset and the desktop window ask for (`tomori_render::profiler::FEATURES`), each eye's pass is timed from its start to its end, and on a desktop, within the pass, each layer of it, the scenery's opaque bodies, Tomori's cubes and controllers, the scenery's translucent surfaces, the panels and signs, and the labels with the blink; on the headset's tiled GPU, which draws a pass's tiles one by one, marks within a pass mean little and are not made, and each eye's pass writes only its start and end, so that every timestamp resolved was written. A frame finding the four readbacks still on their way is not timed. Every frame counts what it drew in each eye: the pieces drawn and culled, the draw calls and the triangles they submit. Once a minute, beside the CPU's line, the headset and the desktop window log at `info`:

```text
GPU over 4320 frames, 4318 timed, ms mean/p95/max: eyes 9.53/10.35/12.10, left 4.77/5.20/6.10, right 4.76/5.18/6.02; an eye: drawn 412.0/530/611, culled 380.2/520/640, draws 61.0/70/78, triangles 31200.0/40211/45102
```

The desktop prefixes it with `Desktop `, and adds each layer's time after the eyes'; a device without timestamps says `no timestamps` in place of the times.

**The edges of each eye are shaded less.** The headset draws each eye with fixed foveated rendering: the middle of its view at every pixel, and towards its edges, which the lenses blur, one fragment for two, four or sixteen pixels, by Vulkan's fragment density map (`VK_EXT_fragment_density_map`, with its non-subsampled images) attached to the eye's render pass, so that its multisampled colour and depth and their resolve into the runtime's image are all drawn at the map's density. The map is the runtime's, by `XR_FB_foveation`, `XR_FB_foveation_configuration`, `XR_FB_swapchain_update_state` and `XR_FB_foveation_vulkan`: the swapchain is made for density maps, and a profile at the level asked for, static rather than dynamic, applied to it. Where the runtime gives no map for the swapchain's images, it is Tomori's own, in rings like the runtime's about where each eye looks straight ahead, shaped from the first frame's fields of view. The level is high unless the intent extra `tomori.foveation` says `none` (or `off`), `low`, `medium` or `high`, optionally with `:own` for Tomori's own map, as `am start -e tomori.foveation low`; `TOMORI_FOVEATION` says the same to `tomori --xr`. Tomori's map at high is full within 0.4 of the half-image of where the eye looks straight ahead, a half across to 0.65, a half both ways to 0.9, and a quarter both ways beyond; medium's rings reach 0.05 further out, and low's 0.1. wgpu does not expose density maps, so the device's `vkCreateRenderPass`, `vkDestroyRenderPass` and `vkCreateFramebuffer`, which wgpu-hal calls, are wrapped: a render pass shaped as an eye's, one subpass of one colour attachment in the swapchain's format at the eyes' samples, with depth, drawing one view or both eyes by multiview with nothing else chained, is made with a density map attachment after its own, as are the passes its pipelines are made against, and each of its framebuffers is given the map of the eye's image it draws, or, drawing both eyes in one pass, a view of the map's two layers, each eye drawn at its own, or, drawing anything else, one full everywhere. wgpu makes a frame's framebuffers anew, so the wrapped `vkCreateFramebuffer` adds the map without allocating; it leaves an imageless framebuffer as it is, and each device's own functions are kept for it, since an activity made again in one process makes another. A runtime without the four extensions, as a desktop runtime, or a GPU that cannot draw at a density map, draws as before, and the log says why. The minute's GPU line on the headset ends with the foveation it was drawn at and how many of the eyes' framebuffers have been given a map so far, two a frame, or one drawing both eyes, and how many others of their shape: `; foveation high, Tomori's map (N framebuffers, 0 others)`.

**Both eyes in one pass, where asked for.** The headset draws each eye in a render pass of its own unless the intent extra `tomori.multiview` says `on`, since the Quest 3's GPU draws both in one pass slower (see the rationale). Asked for, and where it can, it draws both eyes in one render pass, by multiview: into the swapchain's two layers at once, through multisampled colour and depth of two layers, transient as each eye's were, with every draw encoded once and each shader choosing its eye's uniforms by its view index. It can where the device has multiview for two views and, multisampled, multisampled targets of two layers (`tomori_render::multiview::FEATURES`), on Vulkan, and where every registered scenery says it draws both views (`Scenery::views`), having made its pipelines for the views its `Target` says; otherwise, as on a desktop runtime without them, it draws each eye in a pass of its own, as before. The log says at startup how the eyes are drawn, and, where both were asked for in one pass, why not: `Drawing each eye a pass, multiview unavailable: the scenery of … draws one view`, or what the device lacks. A vertex shader drawing both views gives a function the fields of an eye's uniform it reads, as no vertex shader gives a function a uniform by value (below). Each eye's uniforms are an array of two in one buffer, the left eye's then the right's, holding its view and projection and where it is, with a few small values beside, such as the haze and the reveal sphere; the lamps and the rest of a scenery's light, the same for both and, with up to 64 lamps, much larger, are a uniform of their own, written once a frame and bound in every eye's pass, so that both eyes are lit alike. Every pipeline drawn in an eye's pass is made for the renderer's views (`tomori_render::multiview::Views`), from shaders written for one view whose marks are rewritten for both, so that one source serves both. What is culled is what each eye's pass culled, against one frustum enclosing both; what is drawn back to front, the signs, panels and walls, is ordered once, from between the eyes, where each eye's pass ordered it from that eye, which differs only between surfaces about as far from either eye, within the order's tie. Sky's clouds are integrated for both eyes in one pass of their own, a layer each. An avatar's scenery that draws one view gives way to the swarm in a renderer drawing both. The intent extra `tomori.multiview` says `on` to draw both in one pass, `off`, the default, to draw each eye in a pass of its own, the left's first, or `right-first`, each in its own with the right's first, so that the GPU line's `left`, its first pass, is the right eye's; `TOMORI_MULTIVIEW` says the same to `tomori --xr`. The desktop window and the spectator draw one view, as before. Each minute's GPU line says how the eyes were drawn, before the foveation, with no time for each eye where one pass draws both: `; both eyes in one pass; foveation high, Tomori's map (N framebuffers, 0 others)`.

**No vertex shader gives a function a uniform by value.** A function a vertex shader calls, directly or not, is given the fields of a uniform it reads, such as an eye's camera, not a struct or an array read from it, whole: the Quest 3's Adreno driver (0837.0.9) answers `vkCreateGraphicsPipelines` with `VK_ERROR_UNKNOWN` for such a shader drawing both eyes by multiview, as it did for Tomori's cubes passing their camera to the function that grows them (#414), and wgpu takes that, without an error of its own, as the device lost, every object made after it invalid. The headset's device logs why it was lost, should it be: `The GPU device was lost (…): …`.

**A frame for every refresh, drawn from that refresh's eyes.** Each headset frame locates both eyes once, with `xrLocateViews` at the predicted display time `xrWaitFrame` gave it, and draws each eye from its own located pose, not the head's middle nor an earlier frame's; the same poses and time go to `xrEndFrame`'s projection layer, and everything placed from the head that frame, the pill, the hand menu, the ray, signs facing the viewer and the presence sent, is placed from those same views. Nothing that stands in the world is moved as the head moves: the pill stays where it was placed (see `interaction.md`), and what the server places from a participant's head is placed once, at the moment it is placed (see `projection.md`). A frame must also be ready by its refresh: the runtime shows its last frame again at a refresh Tomori misses, turned for where the head now looks but not moved for where it now is, since Tomori submits no depth for it to reproject by, so that a head moving from side to side sees what stands in the world swing with it and jump back, the nearer the more, while turning looks smooth. So the CPU's work for a frame and the GPU's each have the display's period, about 13.9 ms at 72 Hz, rather than both together: the frame loop submits an image's commands to the session's queue and releases it to the runtime, which waits on that queue before composing it, without waiting for the GPU to finish them, and waits for the last frame's rendering only before submitting the next, so that at most one frame is on the GPU while the next is prepared: the next frame's eyes' draws are encoded first (`Renderer::encode`), while the GPU still draws the last, and only their submission (`Renderer::submit`) waits for it, so that the GPU, between one frame and the next, waits for a submission rather than for the CPU to encode a frame. The frame log says how many refreshes were missed each minute.

**A panel too small to read is drawn as its title.** The frame step's projector draws the face of a panel in a wall's slot as its title alone only where its text could not be resolved: where a row of its text, twelve of its font pixels at its size on its wall, its wall's text scale (its slots' scale by its content scale) and a code panel's smaller font pixel included, subtends less than 0.09° from the eye (`projection::faces::FAR`), about 2.2 of a Quest 3's pixels at the centre of its lenses, where it has about 25 to a degree, 0.04° each, and fewer away from it, so that its twelve rows of font pixels fall on two or three of the display's and its glyphs could be no more than a blur. A standard slot's text is drawn as its title beyond about 14.5 m, a wall's of 1.22 times the scale beyond about 17.7 m and of 2.26 times beyond about 33 m, and a code panel's in a standard slot beyond about 10 m. Drawn as its title, it is three times as large as its text (`FAR_SCALE`), bold, on its face's plate and in its frame, at its slot's size to within half a font pixel, and nothing of it is laid out: what it shows changing, its version or its live data, draws nothing and takes no turn, and only its title or its frame changing draws its face again, a handful of glyphs. It is drawn whole again as its rows subtend a fifth more (`NEAR`), 0.108°, so that a head swaying at the threshold does not switch it back and forth, laid out then, at most two a frame, the nearest first (`NEARING`), so that walking up to a wall never lays out all of its panels in one frame. A panel floating, carried or let go, and a volume, whose diagram stands before its face, are always drawn whole. While a face is drawn as its title, the ray points at none of its items, chips or readouts, since none is drawn. A projector made by default draws every face whole, as a still picture wants. **The level of detail can be turned off**, so that a frame's cost can be measured with it and without (`projection::PanelLod`): by the headset's intent extra `tomori.panel_lod`, as `am start -e tomori.panel_lod off`, by `TOMORI_PANEL_LOD=off` for `tomori --xr` and the desktop window, and by `panel_frames` alike, `angle`, the default, or `off`, which draws every panel whole however far, all at once as it is turned off; a value not understood keeps `angle` with a warning. Each client logs its state at `info` as it starts, such as `Panel level of detail: angle, a wall's panel drawn as its title once a row of its text subtends under 0.090°, whole again over 0.108°`, and each minute's CPU line ends its counts with it, beside the panels drawn, culled and drawn as titles a frame (above). Whether the level of detail is kept at all is to be decided by measuring on a Quest 3 (see Validation).

**A few panels a frame.** The frame step's projector is paced (`Projector::paced`): of the panels the server sent or changed, it lays out only a few a frame. Of those that show something already, it lays out again, or draws again for their live data, at most two a frame (`projection::faces::LAYOUTS`): one whose content changed at a new version, or whose age shows otherwise, and a chart whose data moved on; each shows what it showed until its turn, and points at none of its items meanwhile, since its lines are no longer the panel's, so that no tick names another item than the one seen. Of those that show nothing yet, panels that arrive, on entering a space or as they are projected, it lays out at most eight a frame (`projection::faces::ARRIVALS`), each standing from the frame it is laid out in. Of those that arrive together, more than a frame lays out, those nearest the viewer stand first, by where their wall or floating pose is, each in turn among those as near. The rest wait for the frames after, each in turn from where the last frame ended, in the projection's order, so that none waits behind the same ones every frame. So a page of an environment's 128 panels arriving stands within 8 frames, a headset's 180 on entering a space within 23, and the 900 a space may show a client that takes them all within 113, about a second and a half; and its charts or lists changing on one tick never cost one frame all their layout, all showing within 16 frames, about a quarter of a second. What this client changed of a panel itself, a tick shown at once, is laid out in its frame and takes no turn, as is a panel sized anew, carried or on a wall being changed, and everything from a server that versions nothing. A projector made by default lays out every panel in the frame it arrives or changes, as the console, a still picture and a benchmark want.

## Rationale

On a Quest 3 in November 2026, at `9738a1c`, the maintainer raised the hand menu in front of a projection panel and it looked behind it, and found the status pill's text drawn over a panel in front of the pill. Panels, labels and signs were translucent and wrote no depth, so between two of them the order they were drawn in decided, not their distance: the hand menu's labels were drawn before the panels, and the pill after the space's walls and panels. Writing each backing's depth once it is drawn makes distance decide, wherever a surface is, without sorting every surface of every kind against every other for each eye each frame; drawing the hand menu's labels last keeps them blending over the panels behind them. Glyphs and their blending are unchanged, so text is as sharp as before. The tie keeps the frame step's choices where surfaces are about as far away: a panel carried over a wall's slot stands as far away as the panel in it, nearly in its plane, and the hint below a panel carried faces the eyes, so that seen from above its top leans back under the panel's lower edge by about 5 cm at 2.3 m. The 60% threshold makes every panel's face, the label, the hint, the pill and workers' signs hide what is behind them, and leaves the walls' boards and the outline of a slot see-through.

In November 2026, on a Quest 3, the maintainer reported that "the worker signs' transparency is wrong": "where a sign has a rounded corner (the transparent part of its quad), it still writes depth, knocking out whatever is drawn after it behind it", so that with a wall behind a sign they could "see the fence (the background) through the wall, in a halo around the sign's perimeter". The 60% threshold had decided per surface whether a backing wrote depth, and a sign's plate then wrote it over the whole of each of its quads, its transparent rounded corners and margins with them, while the wall's translucent board, drawn after every sign, failed the depth test there. Deciding per fragment, by the same threshold, keeps what was opaque enough to hide what is behind it doing so, exactly, and leaves what is transparent see-through, for every shape and glyph rather than only signs. Drawing signs among the walls and panels by distance from each eye, rather than every sign first, also blends a translucent wall and a sign in the right order either way about; merging the two lists, each already in order, costs nothing per surface, where sorting every surface of every kind against every other would.

In November 2026 the maintainer, seeing seams in a table rule drawn from glyphs, agreed that shapes should be drawn as geometry throughout: a table's header rule and the lines between its columns were a translucent glyph a cell, each grown to join its neighbours, so that every join was brighter, which #247 fixed by drawing each run as one quad, and the maintainer asked that every line, box, bar, tick mark or indicator be drawn the same way, from primitives every panel, menu and card shares, so that they stay crisp and consistent as panels curve, scale, scroll and wrap. Rules, quote bars, the lines and rule of tables, checkboxes, status dots, scroll and time bars, speakers' bars, frames, and result cards' rule and signs' status symbols are now shapes. Thin lines are drawn across from one column of a wedge, a band that widens along its length, and frames from a square frame, so that the field is continuous across them: drawn from a square's two edges, a line at a distance lost and gained up to four times its light as it moved, as the samples the shader averages across a pixel read past where the two met.

Later in November 2026 the maintainer found the hinge on a wall's handle drawn about a tenth of the handle right of and below its middle. Its shapes had been placed from the canvas's top left corner, where every shape is placed inside the padding, and a glyph's ink sits on its baseline, not in the middle of its cell, so arrows and a plus drawn as text sat a little low or high on their handles. A handle's symbol is an icon, and is drawn as shapes, centred on its handle: the edges' arrows, the steppers' minus and plus, the hinge, the padlock and the reset's arrow; the knob's dot, whose glyph is centred in its cell, stays a glyph. A symbol is checked by its ink, on lavapipe, rather than by where it is placed, so that a font, a padding or a placement that moves it shows.

Later in November 2026, to draw an environment's live charts on walls, the maintainer expected a new rendering layer to be needed. The distance-field approach already draws a line at any angle: `Shape::Rule` is a quad turned along its line, sampling one column of the wedge across it, and the render tests show a zigzag of thin chart lines at many angles as steady at a distance as a rule. So the layer is a layout rather than a pipeline, `tomori_scene::chart`, with a solid quad for areas, which a rule or box could not draw, and, once a review found the design's pressure chart over the 12,000 vertices a wall panel's buffer holds, cutting its text, a stroke and a dot of one piece each, where a rule takes three and a disc nine, and labels placed off the cell grid, so that axis values stand level with what they label; a separate pipeline drawing segments as oriented quads with a signed distance in their own shader would have duplicated the rule's antialiasing, needed its own depth and eye handling and a draw call per panel, and drawn nothing the rule does not.

In November 2026, raising the frame limit (#284), an agent found that each full panel cost the headset about 0.7 ms of layout a frame, so that a projection at today's limits of 90 panels would take about 60 ms of each frame there, while the server could hold ten times as many, and the headset's caps were kept. The maintainer agreed: "we should be caching rendering for static frames." Every frame, the projector parsed and wrapped each text panel's Markdown two to four times, to size it, to find how far it scrolls, to meet it with the ray and to draw its face, and the renderer compared each face whole with the one before, and built again the mesh of any drawn at another place in the order, as a panel added before the others moves every one after it. In a headless benchmark on a desktop, `tomori-render`'s `panel_frames` example, at today's limits, the projector and uploads took 6.5 ms a frame in the release profile and 9.6 ms in the dev profile the headset is sideloaded in, and take 0.06 to 0.1 ms and 0.1 to 0.2 ms with each face kept; drawing both eyes, about 0.45 ms and 3.4 ms, is unchanged, and is now most of what a panel costs. Recolouring a frame in the mesh kept, rather than drawing a highlight over the face, keeps one draw a panel and its pixels exactly those of the face built afresh. Scrolling draws the face again from its layout, rather than offsetting the mesh and clipping it, since a table's pinned header and a code panel's whole lines change what shows, not only where, and only one panel scrolls at once, at most 15 rows a second. A mesh in the panel's plane, rather than each face rendered into a texture, keeps text as sharp at every distance as before (see `text.md`), with no mipmaps to blur it, and costs no memory but its vertices.

On a Quest 3 in November 2026, at `b74e9e6`, the maintainer, standing for the first time, found that moving their head from side to side made everything shake "as if the translation of my head is bouncing back and forth between the previous position and the next position", the status pill and near panels the most and far walls less, while turning was fine. The eyes' poses were consistent: one locate a frame, at its predicted display time, each eye its own, and the same poses submitted. The runtime's own log showed the cause: Tomori submitting 51 to 59 frames a second of the display's 72, with about 20 a second shown again stale, while its GPU took about 7.5 ms a frame and the CPU and GPU together about 19 ms. A frame shown again is reprojected for rotation alone without depth, so a world-locked surface moved with the head for a refresh and jumped back, by the head's movement over its distance. The frame loop waited for the GPU to finish each frame before releasing its image, a cautious bridge OpenXR does not require, so that the CPU's work, then about 11 ms with a farmyard of panels each laid out every frame (which #286 addresses), and the GPU's were added together. Overlapping them gives each the whole period. Submitting depth, so that the runtime could reproject a missed frame for the head's movement too, would hide a late frame rather than make it on time, and is left until a frame cannot be made on time.

In November 2026, raising the panels an environment publishes from 8 to 32 (`projection.md`), an agent measured what the panels an environment changes together cost the frame they arrive in, since its charts are polled together and their next points arrive in one patch, and its panels are put up together: on a desktop, an Apple M-series, in the profile the tests build, drawing a chart's face again took about 0.2 ms for a pressure chart of 181 points and 1.3 ms for the largest chart there can be, nearly all of it laying the chart out as shapes, its mesh 0.03 to 0.08 ms more, and laying out, drawing and meshing a list of 30 rows about 0.2 ms, three times that the first time its glyphs are drawn. Eight together had been under 2 ms of one frame; 32 are 7 ms, charts or lists, and 41 ms for charts at their largest, on a headset several times that, where a frame is 11 to 14 ms and every refresh needs one. At first only charts' data was spread over frames; a review found that the 32 panels arriving as a space is entered, or an environment's lists all changing on one tick, still landed in one frame. Panels the server changes are therefore laid out two a frame, in turn, which costs each frame what two cost, under half a millisecond on a desktop for lists and typical charts, and a wall of 32 a ripple of about a quarter of a second, far less than the second between an environment's changes. Panels that arrive were first laid out two a frame as well, which a second review found left a client that takes all of a space's 900 panels filling for 450 frames, six seconds: a panel that shows nothing yet has nothing to go on showing, so waiting costs more than a frame a little longer as a space appears, and they are laid out eight a frame, four times as many, under 2 ms a frame on a desktop for typical panels and 6 ms for 30 full lines, so that 900 stand within a second and a half and a headset's 90 within a sixth of a second. A count was chosen for both rather than a time, as below, and panels are laid out in turn from where the last frame ended, rather than from the first each frame, so that at any frame rate none waits behind the same ones, and in the projection's order rather than nearest or looked at first, which would need each panel placed before it is laid out. A budget of time was not used, since frames would then differ from run to run, and tests with them. A participant's own tick is not spread, since it must show at once, nor a panel sized anew as a wall's handle is dragged, which the participant causes and watches. Pacing is the frame step's rather than every projector's, so that a console, a still picture and the render tests still see every panel in the frame they ask for. None of this is yet measured on a headset: the numbers are a desktop's.

Later in November 2026, raising the panels an environment publishes from 32 to 128 (`projection.md`), an agent measured what more panels cost a frame with `panel_frames`, on an Apple M4 Pro in the release profile, its projector paced as the frame step's is, the Tomori wall, twelve panels floating and an environment's walls of six slots in rings 4, 7 and 10 m and more about home, fourteen to a ring, every slot full: 54 panels for 32 published, 84 for 64, 150 for 128 and 402 for 64 walls all full. The projector and uploads stayed about 0.1 to 0.2 ms a frame for 150 panels and the drawing of both eyes' 179 sheets about 0.4 to 0.5 ms, within the spread of a machine loaded by other builds, over seven interleaved rounds; the far faces' test costs each panel a few multiplications a frame. Drawing far faces as their titles was chosen over refreshing them less often, since a face kept costs nothing until it changes, and its vertices, which are drawn every frame, are what grows. Not yet measured on a headset.

Later in November 2026, in a downstream environment whose walls were 22% larger than standard and whose deck terminals stood a few metres away, the maintainer found panels drawn as titles alone at ordinary viewing distances, though their text would have been readable: at a quarter of a degree a row, six of a Quest 3's pixels, the level of detail drew a standard slot's text as its title from about 5.2 m, nearer than many walls are read from. In November 2026, on a Quest 3 at 72 Hz, in a downstream environment of four large walls and about 22 panels, frames dropped to about 51 a second, 19 to 25 a second shown again stale, while the participant turned their head, with the GPU at 3.4 to 8.9 ms a frame and the CPU and GPU together 23 to 29 ms: the CPU was the bound. In November 2026 the maintainer, finding the farmyard's geometry growing, asked for "a proper rendering system", with a better querying mechanism for rendering and frustum culling as a first step, and approved the proposal of a render world (`docs/proposals/rendering-architecture.md`, #374), whose stage 1 is culling, the sky's order and a profiler. Its census found 14 to 44% of the farmyard's triangles in either eye's view facing a viewpoint's subject, and its measurements on a desktop that drawing the sky first, over every pixel, cost 12 to 16% of the scenery's GPU time. One frustum enclosing both eyes is one test where two would nearly double the work, and conservative, so that culling never removes what either eye sees. In November 2026, at 72 Hz with 4× multisampling and fixed foveation off, the headset's eyes took 6.9 ms of the GPU's frame on average and 10.4 ms at the 95th percentile, of 13.9 ms, mostly in pixels, and the approved proposal's fifth stage is fixed foveation (`docs/proposals/rendering-architecture.md`). Meta's foveation extensions alone, on Vulkan, foveate only the compositor's resampling; the GPU shades fewer of Tomori's own fragments only with the density map attached to its own pass, which one implementer measured as a quarter less of the GPU busy on a Quest 3. wgpu 30 makes Vulkan render passes and framebuffers itself, with no density map; patching its Vulkan backend, carried as a `[patch]` until upstream has one, or beginning the eyes' pass through wgpu-hal, would each have been larger, and the second would have taken the scenery's and every surface's draws out of wgpu's passes. Wrapping three of the device's functions, which Tomori already loads itself for the runtime's device, keeps wgpu as it is, at the cost of relying on its making classic render passes and framebuffers with their attachments: a wgpu that begins passes by dynamic rendering, or with imageless framebuffers, would not be foveated, which the GPU line's count of framebuffers shows as 0. The map belongs to the pass rather than an image, so the transient multisampled attachments need none of their own, and the resolve writes every pixel of the runtime's image. Tomori's own map is there because one implementer found a Quest's runtime giving no density map images while another engine's found it giving them; `:own` compares the two. On the maintainer's Quest 3 the runtime gave none, so every level drew with Tomori's map, and each eye's framebuffers were given it. One pass for both pays such costs once, and cannot be split by eye. wgpu makes neither transient attachments nor, without `MULTISAMPLE_ARRAY`, multisampled ones of two layers, so the multisampled colour and depth are made through wgpu-hal on Vulkan, in lazily allocated memory as wgpu makes a transient attachment of one layer, rather than taking about 190 MB of the headset's memory for attachments its tiles never store. One density map for both eyes, each texel as dense as the denser eye's, so that more neighbouring bins are drawn at the same density in both layers and merged, saved only 0.05 ms (8.76 ms rather than 8.86 and 8.79), and is not kept.
